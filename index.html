Lesson 1.2: Translations

Slide
(x,y)→(x+a,  y+b)(x,y)\rightarrow(x+a,\;y+b)
➕ Right / Up
Right aa: add to xx. Up bb: add to yy.
➖ Left / Down
Left: subtract from xx. Down: subtract from yy.
🔒 Stays the same
Side lengths, angles, parallel lines, orientation. Image ≅\cong preimage.
🔎 Find the rule
a=x′−xa = x' - x and b=y′−yb = y' - y (image minus preimage).
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Changing yy when the shift is left/right (left/right only changes xx).
  • Subtracting backwards: the rule is image − preimage, not preimage − image.
  • Forgetting the sign: "3 left, 2 down" is (x−3,  y−2)(x-3,\;y-2).
🎯 I can use coordinates to describe the effect of a translation on a figure.

🎬 Watch the lesson video

Lesson 1.2: Translations · open on Google Drive

📘 Key rules

  • The original figure is the preimage; the result is the image, written with primes: A→A′A\rightarrow A'.
  • General rule:
    (x,y)→(x+a,  y+b)(x,y)\rightarrow(x+a,\;y+b)
    a>0a>0 → right, a<0a<0 → left, b>0b>0 → up, b<0b<0 → down.
  • A translation is a rigid motion (isometry): it keeps lengths, angle measures, parallel lines and orientation.

🖼️ Picture it

Rule: (x, y) → (x + 3, y + 3). Blue = preimage, orange dashed = image. Every point moves 3 right and 3 up.

✏️ Worked example

Point T(2,−1)T(2,-1) is moved 4 units left and 3 units up. Find T′T'.
Left 4 → subtract 4 from xx; up 3 → add 3 to yy:
(x,y)→(x−4,  y+3)(x,y)\rightarrow(x-4,\;y+3)
T(2,−1)→T′(2−4,  −1+3)=T′(−2,  2)T(2,-1)\rightarrow T'(2-4,\;-1+3)=T'(-2,\;2)

Lesson 1.3: Reflections

Flip
x-axis:(x,−y)y-axis:(−x,y)x\text{-axis}: (x,-y)\qquad y\text{-axis}: (-x,y)
↕️ Across x-axis
(x,y)→(x,−y)(x,y)\rightarrow(x,-y) — yy changes sign.
↔️ Across y-axis
(x,y)→(−x,y)(x,y)\rightarrow(-x,y) — xx changes sign.
↗️ Across y = x
(x,y)→(y,x)(x,y)\rightarrow(y,x) — swap. Across y=−xy=-x: (−y,−x)(-y,-x).
📏 Lines x = h, y = k
x=hx=h: (2h−x,  y)(2h-x,\;y). y=ky=k: (x,  2k−y)(x,\;2k-y).
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the axes: reflecting across the x-axis changes the y-coordinate.
  • Forgetting that a reflection reverses orientation (clockwise ↔ counterclockwise).
  • The line of reflection is the perpendicular bisector of PP′‾\overline{PP'} — use the midpoint.

📘 Key rules

  • A reflection flips a figure over a line of reflection. Each point and its image are the same distance from the line.
  • x-axis:(x,y)→(x,−y)y-axis:(x,y)→(−x,y)\text{x-axis}:(x,y)\rightarrow(x,-y)\qquad \text{y-axis}:(x,y)\rightarrow(-x,y)
    y=x:(x,y)→(y,x)y=−x:(x,y)→(−y,−x)y=x:(x,y)\rightarrow(y,x)\qquad y=-x:(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,-x)
  • Across a vertical line x=hx=h: (x,y)→(2h−x,  y)(x,y)\rightarrow(2h-x,\;y). Across a horizontal line y=ky=k: (x,y)→(x,  2k−y)(x,y)\rightarrow(x,\;2k-y).
  • Reflections keep lengths and angles (rigid motion) but reverse orientation.

🖼️ Picture it

Rule: (x, y) → (−x, y). Reflection across the y-axis: x changes sign, y stays the same. The image is a mirror image.

Turn
90∘ CCW:(x,y)→(−y,x)90^\circ\text{ CCW}: (x,y)\rightarrow(-y,x)
↺ 90° CCW = 270° CW
(x,y)→(−y,  x)(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,\;x)
🔃 180° (either way)
(x,y)→(−x,  −y)(x,y)\rightarrow(-x,\;-y)
↻ 90° CW = 270° CCW
(x,y)→(y,  −x)(x,y)\rightarrow(y,\;-x)
🧭 Default direction
Counterclockwise about the origin unless the problem says clockwise.
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Swapping xx and yy but forgetting to change a sign.
  • Using the CCW rule when the question says clockwise.
  • Thinking rotations change size — they keep lengths, angles and orientation.

📘 Key rules

  • A rotation turns a figure around a fixed point (the center of rotation), usually the origin.
  • 90∘ CCW (=270∘ CW):  (x,y)→(−y, x)90^\circ\text{ CCW } (=270^\circ\text{ CW}):\;(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,\,x)

Lesson 2.2: Dilations

Resize
(x,y)→(kx,  ky)(x,y)\rightarrow(kx,\;ky)
📈 Enlargement
k>1k>1 — the figure gets bigger.
📉 Reduction
0<k<10<k<1 — the figure gets smaller.
🧮 Scale factor
k=image lengthpreimage lengthk=\dfrac{\text{image length}}{\text{preimage length}}
📐 What changes?
Lengths & perimeter ×k\times k; area ×k2\times k^2. Angles stay the same.
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Multiplying the area by kk instead of k2k^2.
  • Writing kk upside down — it is new ÷ old.
  • Adding kk to the coordinates instead of multiplying.
🎯 I can use coordinates to describe the effect of a dilation on a figure.

🎬 Watch the lesson video

Lesson 2.2: Dilations · open on Google Drive

📘 Key rules

  • A dilation resizes a figure from a center of dilation (usually the origin) by a scale factor kk:
    (x,y)→(kx,  ky)(x,y)\rightarrow(kx,\;ky)
  • k=length in imagelength in preimagek=\frac{\text{length in image}}{\text{length in preimage}}
    k>1k>1 enlargement, 0<k<10<k<1 reduction, k=1k=1 same size.
  • Preserved: angle measures and shape. Changed: side lengths and perimeter (×k\times k), area (×k2\times k^2). The center stays fixed.
  • A dilation is not a rigid motion (unless k=1k=1), but it is a similarity transformation.

🖼️ Picture it

k = 2, center (0, 0). Rule: (x, y) → (2x, 2y). Each image point is twice as far from the origin. Sides double; area becomes 4 times as big.

✏️ Worked example

Dilate N(−2,6)N(-2,6) by k=12k=\frac12 with center at the origin.
Multiply both coordinates by 12\frac12:
N′(12⋅(−2),  12⋅6)=N′(−1, 3)N'\left(\tfrac12\cdot(-2),\;\tfrac12\cdot 6\right)=N'(-1,\,3)
Since k<1k<1, this is a reduction.

Lesson 2.3: Similar Figures

Same shape
DEAB=EFBC=DFAC=k\dfrac{DE}{AB}=\dfrac{EF}{BC}=\dfrac{DF}{AC}=k
📐 Angles
Corresponding angles are congruent (≅\cong).
⚖️ Sides
Corresponding sides are proportional (same ratio kk).
🔁 Sequence
Rigid motions + at least one dilation map one figure onto the other.
🟦 Perimeter & area
Perimeter ratio =k=k; area ratio =k2=k^2.
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Matching the wrong sides — follow the letter order in △ABC∼△DEF\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF.
  • Using kk (not k2k^2) for area or paint/surface cost.
  • Assuming all isosceles triangles are similar — their angles can differ.
🎯 I can determine whether two figures are similar using transformations.

🎬 Watch the lesson video

Lesson 2.3: Similar Figures · open on Google Drive

📘 Key rules

  • The symbol ∼\sim means "is similar to". Similar figures have the same shape but can be different sizes.
  • If △ABC∼△DEF\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF:
    ∠A≅∠D,  ∠B≅∠E,  ∠C≅∠F\angle A\cong\angle D,\;\angle B\cong\angle E,\;\angle C\cong\angle F
    DEAB=EFBC=DFAC=k\frac{DE}{AB}=\frac{EF}{BC}=\frac{DF}{AC}=k
  • Two figures are similar exactly when a sequence of rotations, reflections, translations and dilations maps one onto the other.

🖼️ Picture it

△ABC ~ △DEF: every side of △DEF is 2.5 times the matching side of △ABC, and the angles match.

✏️ Worked example

k=104=2.5k=\frac{10}{4}=2.5, so the long side is 2.5×6=15 cm2.5\times6=15\text{ cm}.

Lesson 3.2: Special Cases

How many solutions?
x=a∣a=b  (false)∣a=a  (true)x=a\quad|\quad a=b\;(\text{false})\quad|\quad a=a\;(\text{true})
1️⃣ One solution
Different xx-coefficients → ends as x=numberx=\text{number}.
🚫 No solution
Same xx-coefficient, different constants → false statement like 4=−24=-2.
♾️ Infinitely many
Both sides identical → true statement like 5=55=5.
📊 Graph view
Intersecting lines / parallel lines / the same line.
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Reading 0=00=0 as "x=0x=0" — it means infinitely many solutions.
  • Reading x=0x=0 as "no solution" — x=0x=0 is one solution.
  • Not simplifying both sides completely before deciding.
🎯 I can determine whether a linear equation has one solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions.

🎬 Watch the lesson video

Lesson 3.2: Special Cases · open on Google Drive

📘 Key rules

  • Simplify both sides into the form Ax+B=Cx+DAx+B=Cx+D.
  • If A≠CA\neq C: one solution (x=ax=a). The graphs of each side cross at one point.
  • If A=CA=C and B≠DB\neq D: no solution (you get a false statement such as 0=80=8). Parallel lines.
  • If A=CA=C and B=DB=D: infinitely many solutions (an identity such as 5=55=5). Same line.

🖼️ Picture it

Treat each side as a line: they cross once, never meet (parallel), or are the same line.

⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Adding the adjacent interior angle in the Exterior Angle Theorem — use the two remote ones.
  • Stopping at xx when the question asks for the angle measure.
  • Forgetting to check the three angles add to 180∘180^\circ.
🎯 I can find unknown angle measures using the Triangle Angle-Sum and Exterior Angle Theorems.

Lesson 4.1: Triangle Angles · open on Google Drive

📘 Key rules

  • Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem:
    m∠A+m∠B+m∠C=180∘m\angle A+m\angle B+m\angle C=180^\circ
  • Exterior Angle Theorem: an exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote (non-adjacent) interior angles:
    m∠ACD=m∠A+m∠Bm\angle ACD=m\angle A+m\angle B
  • Linear pair:
    m∠BCA+m∠ACD=180∘m\angle BCA+m\angle ACD=180^\circ
  • A triangle can have at most one obtuse or right angle.

🖼️ Picture it

Exterior angle ∠ACD (red) = ∠A + ∠B (the remote interior angles). ∠BCA + ∠ACD = 180°.

✏️ Worked example

Two angles of a triangle are 50∘50^\circ and 60∘60^\circ. Find the third angle and the exterior angle next to it.
Third angle: 180∘−(50∘+60∘)=70∘180^\circ-(50^\circ+60^\circ)=70^\circ. Exterior: 50∘+60∘=110∘50^\circ+60^\circ=110^\circ (check: 70∘+110∘=180∘70^\circ+110^\circ=180^\circ ✓).
